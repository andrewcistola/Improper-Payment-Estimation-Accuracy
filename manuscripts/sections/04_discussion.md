## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 Principal findings

In five synthetic populations designed after the five CMS improper payment measurements, this study compared the expansion, ratio, and model-assisted regression estimators with audit-finding simulation in its plug-in and parametric-bootstrap versions. Five findings stand out.

First, the plug-in simulation interval is invalid in every program. Because the audit is never resampled, the interval measures only how the audited rates scatter across the population, as opposed to how much those rates would change with a different audit; it covered the true gross rate in 25% of audit years when 95% was intended, and its width shrinks toward zero as the population grows relative to the audit. This is the single-imputation problem of Rubin (1987) in a new setting, and it is not a tuning problem that more iterations can fix.

Second, redrawing the audited error and partial counts from their binomial sampling distributions before each iteration restores coverage of the gross rate: 121 of 125 audit years over the five programs, and 90 to 99 of 100 in the longer calibration run. The correction is a few lines of code and requires no additional record review. In Part C, Part D, PERM, and EIPM the resulting interval is still 5–12% narrower than the simulation estimator's own sampling variability, because the error sizes are held fixed and the rates of different donor kinds are redrawn independently; in CERT it is 13% wider.

Third, the simulation is more precise than the analytic estimators where the payment rules are nonlinear or the error dollars are heavy-tailed, and the gain is real rather than an artifact of a short interval. Over 100 audit years, the standard deviation of the simulation's point estimate was 23% below the ratio estimator's in PERM, 35% below in CERT, and 72% below in Part D; in Part C and EIPM the gain was 3–4%, and most of the narrower interval there reflects the under-coverage of the second finding. The gain comes from the simulation's use of the payments, HCCs, benefit positions, and APTC inputs of every population unit, which makes it a model-based estimator in the sense of Valliant, Dorfman and Royall (2000): it borrows only the audited error counts and takes every unit's error size from the population.

Fourth, that model carries one central assumption, which this study states as such: within each donor kind, error incidence is independent of payment size. The base populations satisfy it by construction. When they were rebuilt so that error probability rises with payment size within strata, the simulation's point estimate ran low by 3% in Part C, 8% in EIPM, 14% in PERM, 22% in CERT, and 65% in Part D, and its interval covered the truth in 24, 21, 6, 0, and 0 of 25 audit years; when error probability falls with payment size, it ran high by 9% in Part C, 7% in PERM, 12% in CERT, and 148% in Part D, with coverage of 18, 18, 0, and 0. The design-based estimators were unaffected: the ratio estimator covered in 22 to 25 of 25 audit years in every scenario. A design-based estimator pays for an omitted variable in variance; a model-based estimator pays in bias, and because the simulation's interval is narrow the bias is not visible from the interval itself. The sign of the association does not matter, and a negative association, which the author's experience of these programs suggests is the more common pattern, overstates the improper payment rate.

Fifth, the assumption can be checked from the audit alone, and where it fails there is a partial remedy. Post-stratifying the simulation's donor kinds on payment decile within each design stratum removed the bias in PERM at almost no cost in precision (bias −14% → −4% and +7% → +0.4%; standard deviation 0.32 → 0.35 points against 0.43 for the ratio estimator). In CERT and Part D, where payments within a stratum span two orders of magnitude and most of the improper dollars sit in the top decile, the deciles removed between half and seven-eighths of the bias (CERT −22% → −5% and +12% → +1.6%; Part D −65% → −38% and +148% → +19%) and gave back most of the precision gain (CERT's standard deviation rose to the ratio estimator's; Part D's doubled but stayed at half of the ratio estimator's). The remedy therefore works where the payment distribution within strata is moderately dispersed and runs out where it is heavy-tailed, which is where a smooth unit-level error model (Section 4.3) is needed instead.

### 4.2 Policy implications

**Recommendation 1 – Adopt parametric-bootstrap audit-finding simulation for PIIA improper payment rates, under a stated and tested assumption.** The simulation with parametric rates produced valid intervals for the gross rate, the most accurate point estimates, and, in Part D, PERM, and CERT, materially narrower intervals than the analytic estimators from the same audit. These properties hold on the assumption that error incidence is independent of payment size within each donor kind. Any Sampling and Estimation Methodology Plan that adopts the method should state this assumption explicitly and show the check in Recommendation 2 each cycle. Plug-in intervals that reuse the audited rates in every iteration should be discontinued wherever they are in use; the Part D interval algorithm is not public and should be reviewed for this feature.

**Recommendation 2 – Verify the assumption every cycle from the audit itself.** The audit contains what is needed. Within each stratum, the audited error rate and the audited taint should be tabulated across payment quantiles (deciles of the population payment distribution within the stratum), with a trend test or a logistic regression of error on log payment with stratum fixed effects, pooled across strata and across cycles where the per-stratum counts are small. The simplest single diagnostic is the comparison the design already permits: the simulation estimate and the design-based ratio estimate from the same audit, stratum by stratum. Audited errors that sit on larger-than-typical units make the ratio estimate exceed the simulation's; the drift is the test, and it costs no additional review. Both estimates should be reported together, with the ratio interval as the design-based benchmark, until a program has several cycles of evidence that the assumption holds.

**Recommendation 3 – Where the check shows an association, post-stratify the donor kinds on payment, and judge the remedy by the payment distribution.** For a program whose payments within strata are moderately dispersed, as in the PERM population here, borrowing findings within stratum × payment decile removes the bias and keeps most of the precision gain, and the simulation can proceed on that basis. For a program whose payments are heavy-tailed within strata, as in CERT and Part D here, the deciles are a partial remedy that gives back most of the precision gain; such a program should report the ratio and simulation estimates together, treat the difference between them as the measure of the assumption's impact, and not reduce its audit on the strength of the simulation until a unit-level error model has been validated.

**Recommendation 4 – Reduce audit sizes only after the assumption has passed and coverage has been confirmed at the smaller size.** If interval width scales with 1/√*n*, the parametric interval would match the current ratio interval at about 83% of the Part C audit, 63% of PERM, 77% of EIPM, and 59% of CERT (Table 4), and from the calibrated standard deviations at about 93%, 60%, 93%, and 42%. In Part D the gain is larger still but rests on the heaviest-tailed population. These are projections (as opposed to demonstrated results), they apply to the gross rate only, and they hold only where Recommendation 2's check passes. Two steps should precede any reduction: confirm coverage directly at the reduced size with a Monte Carlo study on a population built to match the observed error pattern, and check the sparse components (underpayments, technically improper payments) separately, since every method undercovered them.

### 4.3 Future research

Future research should first replace the within-kind independence assumption with a model. A unit-level error model (logistic or zero-inflated regression of error probability and size on log payment, provider type, drug class, or HCC count), fit on the audit and used to assign findings in the simulation, would retain the simulation's use of population structure while removing the assumption that Appendix D shows to be decisive, and would succeed where payment deciles run out, in the heavy-tailed top of the CERT and Part D distributions. Its coverage should be tested on populations built with size-dependent errors, as here. Hierarchical models that pool rates across strata, HCCs, or states would give zero-failure kinds a plausible nonzero rate, and resampling the partial-size list within each iteration, together with a joint rather than independent redraw of correlated rates, would address the 5–12% under-coverage found in the calibration run. BCa or double-bootstrap calibration (Efron 1987; Hall 1988) could be applied to the iteration distribution for sparse components.

Second, future research should identify the audit size at which each program's interval still covers the truth at the nominal rate, by rerunning each design at reduced sizes (for example 50%, 65%, and 80% of current) with at least 200 audit years, for the gross rate and for the components CMS reports. Allocation should be reconsidered at the same time: Neyman allocation minimizes the variance of the analytic estimator (as opposed to the simulation), and the cost of review differs by stratum, so cost-weighted allocation (*n_h* ∝ *N_h S_h* / √*c_h*) belongs in the same study.

Third, the same design should be extended to the settings where interval accuracy carries dollar consequences: state-level PERM eligibility rates, which carry federal match consequences beginning in FY 2030 (P.L. 119-21); State-based Exchange measurement beginning in 2027; and contract-level RADV, where the interval becomes a one-sided lower bound for recovery. Finally, under an appropriate data use agreement, the two assumptions this study had to make, the share of partial errors and the independence of error probability from payment size within strata, should be measured on held-out real audit samples; the second of these is the check in Recommendation 2, applied retrospectively.

### 4.4 Limitations

The major limitation of this study is that its populations are synthetic. They are calibrated only to public aggregates, and the error mechanisms, partial-error shares, and, in the main results, the independence of errors from payment size within strata are assumptions. Appendix D shows that the last assumption decides the simulation's accuracy, and the real degree and direction of dependence in each program are not public. Strata and audit sizes that the agencies do not publish (EIPM, Part C IPM, both Part D audits, and CERT's service-level and jurisdiction strata) are illustrative; MAC jurisdictions, denied claims, and the CERT Part A-to-B rebilling adjustment are not modeled; PERM is one cycle of nine states rather than a three-year rotation of seventeen; Part D's underpayments are structurally sparser than in production.

Second, the main results rest on 25 audit years per program, for which the Monte Carlo standard error of a coverage estimate is 4.4 points, so coverage counts of 22 to 25 cannot be distinguished. The 100-audit-year calibration run (standard error 2.2 points) and the width-to-standard-deviation ratio are the stronger evidence, and they show the parametric interval to be 5–12% too narrow in four programs. The payment-decile results in Appendix D are also 25-audit-year results, and in Part D the fine kinds make the simulation's point estimate noisy enough that its base-population mean is 12% low in these 25 years by chance.

Third, review is assumed to be error-free and final, with no appeals, and every method is given the same perfect findings.

Fourth, the Part D comparison mixes designs and estimators. The ratio and regression comparators use a different audit that reviews about 41,000 PDEs, the plug-in expansion is biased by construction, and the design-based PDE estimators on the same audit, while unbiased, inherit heavy-tailed audited error dollars that make their normal intervals undercover. No analytic method on the 5,000-PDE audit is a clean benchmark, and the Part D width figures should be read with that in mind.

Fifth, the parametric version redraws the rates of different donor kinds independently and holds the audited error sizes fixed, which is why its interval is somewhat too narrow; it is also only one of several ways the audit's uncertainty could be propagated, and payment deciles are only one way to define donor kinds.

Sixth, this study addresses two-sided PIIA rate intervals. Recovery uses rely on one-sided lower bounds, which were not tested, and no CMS program is shown here to use a plug-in simulation; the policy case for discontinuing them is conditional on that being so.

### 4.5 Conclusion

On the question of comparative accuracy, the answer has three parts. Audit-finding simulation with fixed rates produces point estimates as accurate as the analytic estimators but intervals far too narrow to be valid, because the audit is never resampled and the sampling error of the rates is omitted; redrawing the rates from their binomial sampling distribution restores nominal coverage for the gross rate, and in Part D, PERM, and CERT the simulation is then materially more precise than the analytic estimators from the same audit. That precision rests on one assumption that the design-based estimators do not need, that error incidence is independent of payment size within each donor kind; when it fails, the simulation is biased in the direction opposite to the association and its interval does not reveal it. The assumption can be checked from the audit each cycle, and where it fails, borrowing findings within payment deciles removes the bias where payments are moderately dispersed and reduces it where they are heavy-tailed. This study therefore recommends parametric-bootstrap audit-finding simulation for PIIA improper payment rates, with the independence assumption stated in the methodology plan and tested every cycle against the design-based ratio estimate, with payment-decile donor kinds where the test fails, and with any reduction in audit size deferred until the test has passed and coverage has been confirmed at the smaller size.
