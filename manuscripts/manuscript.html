Abstract

Background. The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) estimates improper payments in Medicare Part C, Medicare Part D, Medicaid (PERM), Marketplace advance premium tax credits (EIPM), and Medicare fee-for-service (CERT) from audited samples, and the law requires that each estimate include a 95% confidence interval. The available projection methods include the analytic expansion, ratio, and regression estimators and audit-finding bootstrap simulation (which assigns audited findings across the population and reruns the payment rules). These methods have not been compared against a known truth.

Purpose. The purpose of this study is to compare the accuracy of available methods for estimating improper payments in federal health programs. In this study, accuracy includes both point accuracy and interval accuracy (coverage at the nominal 95% and, after that, width).

Methods. This study built five synthetic populations (one per program) with fabricated errors, calibrated each to public FY 2025 aggregates, and drew each program’s production-like audit (990 to 37,651 units) in 25 independent audit years. Expansion and ratio estimators with normal intervals and a model-assisted regression estimator (models fit within strata on basic beneficiary variables and the amount paid, with a residual interval) were compared against bootstrap simulation (1,000 iterations). The bootstrap simulation either held error rates fixed at their audited values or redrew them each iteration from their binomial sampling distributions (parametric rates).

Results. Bootstrap point estimates were as accurate as or more accurate than the analytic estimates. Fixed-rate bootstrap intervals were 5–21% as wide as the analytic intervals and contained the true gross rate in only 30 of 125 audit-years (24%) because they omit the sampling variance of the audited rates. Parametric-rate intervals contained the true gross rate in 121 of 125 audit-years (96.8%; 23–25 of 25 in every program). These intervals were also 11–21% narrower than the ratio estimator’s in four programs and 63% narrower than the same-audit expansion interval in Part D. Measures built from few errors (for example, underpayments) were undercovered by every method.

Conclusions. Fixed-rate bootstrap intervals should not be used. Bootstrap simulation with parametric rates produces valid and narrower intervals, and this study recommends it for PIIA improper payment rates. At current precision, this method would permit audits roughly 20–38% smaller, which would reduce review costs without sacrificing accuracy (pending confirmation at reduced sizes).

Keywords: improper payments; bootstrap; Monte Carlo simulation; survey sampling; Medicare; Medicaid; payment integrity; confidence intervals

1. Background

1.1 Improper payment measurement in CMS programs

The Payment Integrity Information Act of 2019 (PIIA) requires a statistically valid estimate of improper payments for every federal program at risk of significant improper payments. Appendix C to the Office of Management and Budget (OMB) Circular A-123 sets out how agencies produce this estimate (OMB 2021). The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) runs five such measurements:

  • Medicare Part C (the Part C Improper Payment Measure, for Medicare Advantage risk adjustment);
  • Medicare Part D (the Part D Improper Payment Measure, for prescription drug events);
  • Medicaid and CHIP (Payment Error Rate Measurement, PERM);
  • Marketplace advance premium tax credits (Exchange Improper Payment Measurement, EIPM);
  • Medicare fee-for-service (Comprehensive Error Rate Testing, CERT).

Together, these programs reported more than $90 billion in improper payments for FY 2025 (CMS 2025a). Each measurement follows the same three steps:

  1. Draw a probability sample (the audit).
  2. Establish the payment error for each sampled unit through medical or eligibility review.
  3. Project the sample errors to the program and attach a 95% confidence interval.

The interval is not a formality. Inspectors General review sampling and estimation plans every year, and interval accuracy is an auditable item. For example, the Department of Education Office of Inspector General (OIG) has found estimates unreliable because their confidence intervals were inaccurate (ED OIG 2025). Interval width also sets the cost of measurement. Medical record review is the dominant cost of every program, and audit sizes are chosen to meet precision targets (for example, ±3 percentage points at 95% confidence for CERT; CMS 2025b).

1.2 Analytic estimators and bootstrap simulation

The standard projection methods are design-based estimators with normal-theory intervals (Cochran 1977):

  • Expansion weights each sampled error up to the population.
  • Ratio divides the weighted errors by the weighted payments.
  • Regression fits a model of the error on characteristics known for every unit, predicts the whole population, and adds the weighted sample residuals (model-assisted estimation; Särndal et al. 1992).

The intervals for these estimators rely on a central limit argument that is weakest exactly where improper payment data are found. Errors are rare, zero-inflated, right-skewed, and often all-or-nothing (Neter and Loebbecke 1975; Kvanli et al. 1998; Edwards et al. 2023).

A second family of methods simulates (as opposed to projects). The audit findings (which HCCs failed, which drug claims were wrong and by how much, and which applications were ineligible) are assigned at random to the unaudited units of the whole population, and the program’s payment function is rerun. Repeating this process many times produces a distribution of improper payments. This study calls this approach audit-finding bootstrap simulation. The published Part D interval is already simulation based (CMS 2023, 2025c).

This approach is attractive for three reasons:

  • Nonlinear payments. The simulation reruns the actual payment rules, so effects such as the Part D benefit phases, risk-score hierarchies, and the APTC schedule are handled exactly (as opposed to through a linear approximation).
  • Every measure at once. One set of iterations produces over-, under-, gross, and net payments, along with any subgroup.
  • Traceability. Every simulated dollar can be traced to an audited finding.

To the author’s knowledge, the comparative accuracy of these methods (and in particular the validity of the simulation intervals) has not been tested against a known truth. A companion narrative review describes the design of each program and the evidence on interval accuracy in detail (Cistola 2026).

1.3 Why validity is in question

The audit-finding bootstrap differs from the textbook bootstrap (Efron and Tibshirani 1993; Mashreghi et al. 2016) in one important respect: it never resamples the audit. Each iteration reuses the same audited findings, and the error rates estimated from them, so the spread across iterations reflects only which unaudited units happen to receive errors. The uncertainty in the rates themselves is not represented. However, this is the uncertainty a confidence interval is meant to express, namely what the audit would have found had a different sample been drawn. Section 4.1 shows that this omission is large and predictable.

2. Purpose

The purpose of this study is to compare the accuracy of available methods for estimating improper payments in federal health programs. The methods are:

  • three design-based analytic estimators (expansion, ratio, and a model-assisted regression estimator);
  • audit-finding bootstrap simulation.

In this study, accuracy has two parts:

  • Point accuracy: how close the point estimate is to the true improper payment.
  • Interval accuracy: whether the 95% interval contains the truth in about 95% of repeated audits (coverage) and, among intervals that do, how narrow the interval is (width). A narrower valid interval is more accurate because the same precision can then be achieved with a smaller and less costly audit.

The specific aims of this study are to:

  1. measure the point and interval accuracy of each method in five synthetic populations designed after the five CMS measurements;
  2. explain any failure of the bootstrap simulation interval;
  3. test a parametric alternative that corrects this failure;
  4. determine whether the most accurate method could support smaller audits.

Recovery uses of extrapolation (contract-level RADV demands and provider overpayment demands) are out of scope.

3. Methods

3.1 Study design

This study is a Monte Carlo validation study that follows the design of Edwards et al. (2023):

  1. Population. For each program, a synthetic population is built, and errors are fabricated on it with stated probabilities. The true improper payment is therefore known to the cent.
  2. Audit years. The program’s production audit design is then applied 25 times, each time drawing an independent sample. Each draw is an audit year, so the 25 draws show how each estimator behaves when the audit is repeated.
  3. One audit per year. Each audit year has exactly one audit, and every method uses only that audit. The analytic methods estimate their variance from this audit. The bootstrap simulation assigns the audit findings exactly as audited.

All methods are scored on the same 25 audit years. An interval is counted as containing the truth if lower ≤ θ ≤ upper. With 25 audit years, 24 of 25 is the nearest count to the nominal 95% (23.75), and the Monte Carlo standard error of a coverage estimate near 95% is about 4.4 percentage points.

This study uses no real data. Populations are calibrated only to public aggregate results (Appendix A). Every number in this paper can be regenerated from the public repository (Data and code availability).

3.3 Estimands

Netting and measures. For each program, the payment error is first netted within the payment unit (the enrollee, beneficiary, payment, application, or claim). The measures are then defined as:

  • over = Σ max(y, 0)
  • under = Σ max(−y, 0)
  • gross = over + under
  • net = over − under

Every measure is estimated in dollars with its interval and then divided by the known total payments X. The gross rate is the PIIA estimand and the primary outcome. Because PERM and EIPM errors are all overpayments, their components are reported instead of over and under (eligibility and claims for PERM; technically improper and monetary for EIPM). CERT also reports gross by claim type.

3.4 Analytic estimators

The three analytic estimators use the program’s stratified design. Let N_h, n_h, and w_h = N_h/n_h be the stratum sizes and weights, and let *T̂_y = Σ_h w_h Σ y* and *T̂_x* be the weighted totals. The estimators are:

  • Expansion: *θ̂ = T̂_y / X*.
  • Ratio: *θ̂ = T̂_y / T̂_x*.
  • Regression (model-assisted): Within each model stratum m, the audited y is regressed by design-weighted least squares on an intercept and the unit’s predictors z (basic beneficiary variables plus the amount paid). The fitted models predict every population unit, and the estimate adds the weighted audit residuals: θ̂ = [Σ_U ŷ + Σ_s w (y − ŷ)] / X. The predictors are:
    • Part C: payment, demographic score, months enrolled, and number of HCCs, within risk-score terciles.
    • Part D: Medicare payment, gross drug cost, number of PDEs, and reaching the catastrophic phase, within post-strata (long-term care, low-income subsidy, other).
    • PERM: amount paid, month, and the beneficiary’s number of payments and total paid.
    • EIPM: APTC, age, household size, income, and months.
    • CERT: amount paid (the claims carry no beneficiary variables).
    The model is fit on the same audit as the ratio estimator (for Part D, the 990-beneficiary audit). A stratum with fewer than 10 audited errors, or with too few units for the model, uses an intercept-only model, which is equivalent to expansion within that stratum. This approach follows model-assisted estimation as used in forest inventory (McConville et al. 2020) and model-based extrapolation of random audits in tax-gap estimation (Johns and Rosenbaum 2024).

Variances use the stratified-SRS formula with a finite population correction applied to y (expansion), to the ratio residual y − θ̂x (ratio), or to the model residual y − ŷ (regression), all from the single audit. Expansion and ratio report θ̂ ± 1.96·SE. Regression uses a t multiplier, with degrees of freedom reduced by the strata and model terms fitted.

Two program-specific forms apply:

  • Part C expansion works in risk-score units. The mean audited over- and under-risk-score error in each tercile is multiplied by the tercile’s size, the national mean county rate, and months.
  • Part D expansion applies the audited PDE error rate for each stratum to every PDE in the population, reruns the benefit, and uses a delta-method interval. Each stratum’s rate is moved by ±1 SE, and the benefit is rerun each way.

3.5 Audit-finding bootstrap simulation (fixed rates)

Within each donor kind k, take the n_k audited units from the audit:

  • f_k of them are in error;
  • g_k of those errors are partial, with sizes s_1, …, s_{g_k}.

The donor kind is the sampling stratum. In Part C, the donor kind is the HCC (20 HCC failure rates and 10 family addition rates).

In each of 1,000 iterations:

  1. Every non-audited unit of kind k is in error with probability p_k = f_k/n_k.
  2. Given an error, the unit is partial with probability q_k = g_k/f_k, with its size drawn uniformly from the audited partial sizes. Otherwise, the error is whole.
  3. Audited units keep their own findings.
  4. The payment function is rerun for every unit, and the measures are summed.

Steps 1 and 2 are equivalent to giving every unit the finding of a uniformly drawn audited donor of its kind. These steps only touch units that receive an error, so a 5-million-claim iteration takes milliseconds.

The estimate is the mean of the iteration rates, and the interval is their 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. When every unit is audited, every iteration returns the exact true rate (this property is tested). In Part D, all PDEs of the full population are simulated, and the benefit is rerun for every beneficiary.

3.6 The parametric-rate alternative

The parametric version changes only one thing. Before each iteration, the rates are redrawn from their sampling distribution given the audit:

*p̃_k = Binomial(n_k, p_k)/n_k, q̃_k = Binomial(f_k, q_k)/f_k*

Steps 1–4 then run with *p̃_k* and *q̃_k*. Each iteration therefore represents a different plausible audit outcome in addition to a different assignment. Rates with no audited failures stay at zero, so no failures are extrapolated to HCCs the audit never observed. Partial sizes are still drawn from the audited list.

This version is a parametric bootstrap of the rate parameters (Efron and Tibshirani 1993, ch. 6) applied inside the simulation. This study calls the two versions bootstrap simulation (fixed rates) and bootstrap simulation (parametric rates). The parametric version is not a Bayesian bootstrap and requires no prior.

3.7 Outcomes

For each program, measure, and method, the following outcomes are calculated over the 25 audit years:

  • Coverage: the number of intervals containing the truth, and how many lie entirely above or entirely below it.
  • Width: the median 95% interval half-width, in percentage points of payments and in national dollars.
  • Point accuracy: the median point estimate, and the mean absolute and mean signed error relative to the true rate.

To address the audit-size question, this study compares the parametric interval with the analytic interval from the same audit:

  • the ratio estimator for Part C, PERM, EIPM, and CERT;
  • expansion for Part D (the analytic method that uses the same PDE audit).

If interval width scales with 1/√n, the audit size at which the parametric method would match the analytic width is (w_parametric / w_analytic)² of the current audit.

Before evaluating intervals, it is important to establish what the bootstrap simulation does well. The bootstrap point estimate is at least as accurate as the best analytic estimator in every program. It ties in Part C and is more accurate in the other four programs (Table 3):

The Part D difference reflects the benefit design. A PDE error changes Medicare’s liability by an amount that depends on where the beneficiary sits in the benefit, and only rerunning the benefit captures this effect. The stratum-average rates used by expansion run 5.7% low.

The fixed and parametric versions produce the same point estimate to within 0.2 percentage points of relative error because the binomial redraw is centered on the audited rates. Signed errors for the bootstrap are within ±1% of the truth in every program except EIPM.

In EIPM, every method runs 5–8% low. Technically improper errors are rare (about 9 per audit) and large (the whole APTC), so in most audit years the sample contains fewer than the expected number, and the median estimate falls below the mean. This is a small-sample property of the data (as opposed to a property of any one method). The further 2–3% shortfall for the bootstrap in EIPM comes from moving the income ratio of a monetary error to an application at a different point of the APTC schedule (Appendix B.4).

4.2 The bootstrap interval problem

The fixed-rate bootstrap interval is far too narrow in every program. Its median half-width is 5% to 21% of the half-width of the analytic interval from the same audit (Table 4), and it contains the true gross rate in only 3 to 10 of 25 audit years (Figure 2). The misses fall on both sides about equally. For example, in Part C, 12 intervals lie entirely above the truth and 10 lie entirely below. These results show that the problem is not bias. Rather, the interval is centered correctly and is simply too short.

The cause is structural. The true sampling variance of an estimated improper total T̂ can be written by the law of total variance, conditioning on the audit A:

Var(T̂) = E[Var(T̂ | A)] + Var(E[T̂ | A]).

The fixed-rate bootstrap holds the audit, and therefore the rates p_k and q_k, fixed. Its iterations vary only in which unaudited units receive errors. As a result, its spread estimates only the first term (the reassignment variance). This term grows with the population size N, roughly as N p(1 − p) times the mean squared error size. The second term is the variance of the rates themselves across possible audits, which is the term a confidence interval exists to express. This term grows with N²/n.

The ratio of the two standard deviations is therefore roughly √(n/N), and the observed ratios follow this pattern:

Program √(n/N) Observed fixed-rate width, share of analytic
Part C (990 of 330,253 enrollees) 5.5% 5%
EIPM (2,000 of 142,152 applications) 11.9% 13%
CERT (37,651 of 5.34 million claims) 8.4% 21%
PERM (520 of about 343,000 payments per state) 3.9% 13%
Part D (5,000 of about 4.5 million PDEs) 3.3% 10%

The ratio is higher than √(n/N) in three programs:

  • CERT: Inpatient claims are sampled at 22%, which inflates the reassignment term.
  • PERM and Part D: Error sizes vary more because whole payments of very different amounts can be in error and the benefit is nonlinear.

None of these programs comes close to the analytic width.

More iterations do not solve this problem. Additional iterations estimate the reassignment spread more precisely but do not add the missing term. In addition, the interval becomes narrower as the population grows relative to the audit, which is the opposite of what a sampling interval should do. A larger program with the same audit would report greater precision than a smaller one.

4.3 The parametric alternative

The parametric version adds the missing term directly. Before each iteration, each rate is redrawn from its binomial sampling distribution given the audit (Section 3.6), so the iteration distribution spans plausible audit outcomes in addition to plausible assignments.

This change restores coverage of the gross rate in every program: 24, 25, 24, 23, and 25 of 25 audit years for Part C, Part D, PERM, EIPM, and CERT. In total, this is 121 of 125 audit-years (96.8%), as opposed to 30 of 125 (24%) with fixed rates. No parametric interval for the gross rate lay entirely above the truth, and all four misses were below.

The parametric intervals are also narrower than the analytic intervals from the same audit (Table 4, Figure 1):

In dollars (Appendix Table C1):

  • PERM: The parametric interval is ±$0.90B around a median of $8.46B, as opposed to ±$1.13B for the ratio estimator.
  • CERT: ±$1.24B around $28.81B, as opposed to ±$1.57B.
  • Part C: ±$3.57B around $23.62B, as opposed to ±$4.02B.

The gain in width has two sources:

  • The known structure of the population. The simulation reruns the payment function over the known payment, HCCs, benefit position, or APTC inputs of every unit. The simulation is therefore a model-assisted estimator that uses the auxiliary information of the full population, whereas the ratio estimator uses only total payments.
  • The list of partial sizes is held fixed. The audited list of partial error sizes is reused (as opposed to resampled), which leaves out some uncertainty in error size. The coverage results suggest that this omission is small for the gross rate, but it is a reason to extend the method (Section 6).

The Part D gain is the largest and the least certain. The only analytic method on the same PDE audit is expansion. Its delta-method interval is wide and its point estimate runs low, so the 37% likely understates what a well-specified analytic interval on that audit could achieve.

The exceptions are the sparse measures (those built from a handful of audited errors):

Every method undercovers these measures, and the misses fall mainly below the truth. The interval lies under the truth in audit years whose sample happened to contain few of the rare errors. This is the zero-inflated, right-skewed setting in which normal-theory intervals are known to fail (Kvanli et al. 1998; Hesterberg 2015). The parametric bootstrap does not avoid this problem because a binomial redraw centered on a low audited rate remains low. The gross rate is protected because the sparse component is a small share of it.

4.5 Regression with beneficiary variables

The regression estimator fits a least-squares model within each stratum on the audit used by the ratio estimator, using basic beneficiary variables and the amount paid. It then predicts every population unit and adds the weighted audit residuals. The additional variables changed little: - Part C, PERM, EIPM, and CERT: Coverage of the gross rate was 23, 23, 23, and 24 of 25. Width and point accuracy were within a few percent of those of the ratio estimator. - Part D: Drug cost and reaching the catastrophic phase explain part of the beneficiary payment error, and the interval narrowed from ±0.67 (ratio) to ±0.58 points. However, coverage fell to 15 of 25, and the point estimate ran 7.8% high on average.

With few large errors in a 990-beneficiary audit, a better-fitting model leaves residuals that understate the true sampling variance. This is the overfitting risk of model-assisted estimation with small samples (McConville et al. 2020). In PERM, all but the largest-payment fee-for-service stratum had too few audited errors for a model and used the intercept-only fallback, which is equivalent to expansion within the stratum.

Across the five programs, regression covered the true gross rate in 108 of 125 audit years (86.4%), as opposed to 111 for the ratio estimator and 121 for the parametric bootstrap.

5. Discussion

5.1 Principal findings

In five synthetic populations designed after the five CMS improper payment measurements, audit-finding bootstrap simulation produced point estimates as accurate as, or more accurate than, the expansion, ratio, and regression estimators. However, its fixed-rate interval was invalid in every program, covering the truth in 24% of audit years when 95% was intended. This failure is not a tuning problem. Because the audit is never resampled, the interval measures only how the audited rates scatter across the population (as opposed to how much those rates would change with a different audit). Its width also shrinks toward zero as the population grows. Given these findings, any production use of a fixed-rate audit-finding simulation for PIIA intervals should be reviewed.

Redrawing the rates from their binomial sampling distribution before each iteration corrects the interval with a change of a few lines of code and no additional review. This version covered the true gross rate in 96.8% of audit years. Its intervals were 11–21% narrower than those of the ratio estimator in four programs, and much narrower than those of expansion in Part D.

5.2 Recommendation

This study recommends that CMS improper payment measurements adopt bootstrap simulation with parametric rates for the gross improper payment rate and its interval, with the analytic ratio interval reported alongside it during a transition period. This recommendation rests on three findings:

  1. Validity: coverage at the nominal level for the gross rate in every program.
  2. Accuracy: the most accurate point estimates of the methods studied.
  3. Precision: narrower intervals than the analytic methods from the same audit.

The cost case rests on precision. A narrower interval at the same coverage means that the current precision can be reached with fewer records reviewed. If width scales with 1/√n, the parametric method would match the current width of the analytic interval with about:

Program Share of the current audit Audit size
Part C 79% about 780 enrollees instead of 990
EIPM 80% about 1,600 applications instead of 2,000
PERM 63% about 330 payments per state-year instead of 520
CERT 62% about 23,300 claims instead of 37,651

This represents a 20–38% reduction in medical record and eligibility reviews, which dominate the cost of each measurement. The projected reduction for Part D is larger still but rests on a weaker comparator (Section 4.3).

These figures are projections (as opposed to demonstrated results), and they apply only to the gross rate. Two conditions should precede any reduction:

  • Confirm coverage at the reduced size. Coverage must be confirmed directly at the smaller audit sizes (Section 6).
  • Protect sparse components. Components that CMS reports separately and that rest on few errors (underpayments and technically improper payments) need their own precision check because their coverage was already below nominal under every method.

5.3 Implementation

The parametric version runs on the same audit data as the fixed version. It adds two binomial draws per stratum per iteration, and at 1,000 iterations it ran in seconds (EIPM, Part C) to minutes (CERT, 5.3 million claims) on a two-core machine.

The method is fully specified by the audit and the payment function. It can therefore be written into a Sampling and Estimation Methodology Plan and tested by an Inspector General with the same census check used in this study: auditing every unit must return the true rate exactly. Rates with no audited failures stay at zero. This is a conservative choice that keeps the simulation from inventing errors the audit never observed.

5.4 Limitations

  1. Number of audit years. Coverage was estimated from 25 audit years per program. The Monte Carlo standard error is about 4.4 points, so 23/25 and 25/25 are not statistically distinguishable. The consistency across five programs and 22 measures is the stronger evidence, but a larger replication is needed.
  2. Synthetic populations. The populations are synthetic and calibrated only to public aggregates. It should be clearly noted that:
    • Error mechanisms, partial-error shares, and the independence of errors from payment size within strata are assumptions.
    • Strata the agencies do not publish (EIPM, Part C IPM, and CERT service-level strata) are illustrative.
    • MAC jurisdictions and the CERT Part A-to-B rebilling adjustment are not modeled.
  3. Perfect review. Review is assumed to be error-free and final, with no appeals.
  4. Part D design differences. The Part D analytic comparators use a different audit (990 beneficiaries) or a different interval construction (delta method), so the Part D comparisons mix design and estimator.
  5. Fixed error sizes. The parametric version does not resample the distribution of error sizes. Coverage suggests that this matters little for the gross rate, but it may contribute to the undercoverage of sparse components.
  6. Scope. This study addresses two-sided PIIA rate intervals. Recovery uses rely on one-sided lower bounds, which were not tested.

6. Future research

  1. Optimal audit sizes. The audit-size figures in Table 4 are projections from a 1/√n scaling. Future research should test these projections directly by rerunning each program’s design at reduced audit sizes (for example, 50%, 65%, and 80% of current) with at least 200 audit years. This would identify the smallest audit at which the parametric interval still covers the truth in 95% of audit years, both for the gross rate and for the components CMS reports. Allocation should be reconsidered at the same time. Neyman allocation minimizes the variance of the analytic estimator (as opposed to the variance of the simulation). In addition, the cost of review differs by stratum (for example, an inpatient record costs more to review than a Part B claim), so cost-weighted allocation (n_h ∝ N_h S_h / √c_h) belongs in the same study.
  2. Model + simulation alternatives. The parametric version models only the rates. Future research should consider several natural extensions:
    • Hierarchical Bayesian error models. Beta-binomial rates pooled across strata, HCCs, or states would give zero-failure HCCs and small strata a plausible nonzero rate (as opposed to a fixed zero). Their posterior predictive draws would replace the binomial redraw.
    • Unit-level error models. Logistic or zero-inflated regression of error probability and error size on unit features (for example, payment amount, provider type, drug class, or HCC count) would feed the simulation. This model-assisted microsimulation would use the auxiliary data of the full population and could narrow intervals further where the features predict error.
    • Resampling the size distribution. Partial sizes could be resampled with replacement within each iteration so that uncertainty in error size enters alongside uncertainty in the rates.
    • Calibrated intervals. BCa or double-bootstrap calibration could be applied to the iteration distribution to correct any remaining tail imbalance in sparse components (Efron 1987; Hall 1988).
  3. Settings beyond the PIIA national rate. This study can be extended to:
    • state-level PERM eligibility rates, which carry federal match consequences beginning in FY 2030 (P.L. 119-21);
    • State-based Exchange measurement beginning in 2027;
    • contract-level RADV, where the interval becomes a lower bound for recovery and one-sided coverage matters.
  4. Validation on real audits. Under an appropriate data use agreement, the synthetic results can be checked against held-out real audit samples. In particular, future research should examine the share of partial errors and whether error probability depends on payment size, which this study assumes away within strata.

7. Conclusion

On the question of comparative accuracy, the answer is consistent across the five programs studied. In every program, audit-finding bootstrap simulation with fixed rates produces point estimates as accurate as the analytic estimators but intervals far too narrow to be valid. The cause is structural. Because the audit is never resampled, the interval omits the sampling error of the rates. Redrawing the rates from their binomial sampling distribution before each iteration restores nominal coverage for the gross rate in every program. These intervals are also narrower than the analytic intervals from the same audit in all five programs (11–21% narrower than the ratio estimator in four programs, and much narrower than expansion in Part D). This study recommends parametric-rate bootstrap simulation for improper payment rate estimation. The gain in precision gives CMS room to reduce audit sizes (and with them, review costs) without losing accuracy, subject to direct confirmation at the reduced sizes.

Appendix A. Data process

Every number in this study comes from synthetic populations built from stated probability distributions. This study used no protected health information, no unit-level data from any source, and no non-public CMS material (for example, contractor methodology plans or internal error data). Populations are calibrated only to public aggregate results, namely the FY 2025 HHS Agency Financial Report figures and the program-specific CMS result pages cited in Appendix B.

A.1 Pipeline

Each program folder has two stages, run by one YAML-driven runner (ipsim):

  1. errors/ builds an error-free population, applies fabricated errors, records the truth, and checks it against public figures.
    • Population. Units with their payment amounts and the fields each payment function needs: risk scores and HCCs, PDEs and benefit phases, APTC inputs, and claims.
    • Errors. The candidate error for each unit is drawn first and then turned on with a stated probability. Where a public aggregate is used as a target, the probability comes from one closed-form multiplier (the target dollars divided by the expected dollars of the candidates). No iterative tuning is used.
    • Truth. The true improper amount y and payment x for every unit, and the true over, under, gross, and net totals.
    • Checks. Each population is compared with the public aggregates it is calibrated to. Results are written to checks.json.
  2. estimation/ draws the audits and estimates with each method.
    • Frame and design. The strata, the allocation (Neyman where stated), and the sample size.
    • Audits. 25 independent production audits (“audit years”).
    • Estimators. Expansion and ratio, with normal 95% intervals, written once in shared/sql/lib/02_estimators.sql and tested against an independent numpy implementation. Regression: models fit within strata on beneficiary variables plus the amount paid, predicted for the population, with a residual interval (shared/ipsim/regression.py, predictors from each program’s 30_regression.sql; tested in shared/tests/test_regression.py).
    • Bootstrap simulation. Fixed and parametric rates, 1,000 iterations per audit year, run by the shared loop in shared/ipsim/simulation.py with each program’s kernel.
    • Scoring against the truth.

The file shared/report.yaml joins every estimate to the truth (shared/results/report/by_scenario.csv, by_audit_year.parquet). manuscripts/scripts/build_tables.py produces every table and figure in this manuscript from the parametric-rate results (<program>/estimation/results/, the configs’ default) and the fixed-rate results from the same audits (<program>/estimation/results_fixed/).

A.2 Reproducibility

  • Random numbers. In SQL, every random draw is a hash of (seed, stream, key). In the bootstrap simulation, draws come from PCG64 generators seeded by SeedSequence([scenario seed, audit year]).
  • Exact arithmetic. Money is stored as DECIMAL, and floating-point sums run in a fixed order.
  • Identical reruns. Reruns produce byte-identical files at any thread count. This is tested in shared/tests/test_determinism.py, and manifest.json records a SHA-256 hash for every output.
  • Payment functions. Each program’s numpy payment function is tested to reproduce the SQL improper amount to the cent for every unit when given that unit’s own audit finding. In addition, a census audit is tested. When every unit is audited, both bootstrap versions must return the exact true rates (shared/tests/test_simulation.py).

A.3 Scale

Each synthetic unit stands for w real units:

Program w
Part C 100
Part D 500
PERM 200
EIPM 100
CERT 200

Rates are scale-free. Dollars are reported at national scale: rate × synthetic payments × w.

A.4 Estimands and netting

  • Netting. Errors are first netted within the payment unit (the enrollee for Part C, the beneficiary for Part D, the payment for PERM, the application for EIPM, and the claim for CERT).
  • Measures.
    • Over = Σ max(y, 0)
    • Under = Σ max(−y, 0)
    • Gross = over + under
    • Net = over − under
  • Rates. Each total is first estimated in dollars with its interval and then divided by the known total payments X.
  • Overpayment-only programs. PERM and EIPM have only overpayments, so gross = over = net. Their components are reported instead (eligibility and claims for PERM; technically improper and monetary for EIPM).
  • Analytic estimators. The analytic estimators estimate the rate directly as *T̂_y / X* (expansion), *T̂_y / T̂_x* (ratio) or [Σ_U ŷ + Σ_s w (y − ŷ)] / X (regression, with models fit within strata), using stratified-SRS variance formulas with finite population correction on y or on the residuals.

A.5 Bootstrap simulation, step by step

For one audit year and one iteration:

  1. Rates from the audit. Within each donor kind (the stratum, or the HCC for Part C), from the n audited units:
    • f = how many are in error;
    • g = how many of those errors are partial;
    • the list of audited partial sizes (taints, income ratios, or HCC losses).
  2. Parametric rates only. Redraw p = Binomial(n, f/n)/n and q = Binomial(f, g/f)/f.
  3. Assign errors. Every non-audited unit gets an error with probability p, drawn as a Bernoulli process over the unit list.
  4. Partial or whole. Each error is partial with probability q, and its size is drawn uniformly from the audited partial list. Otherwise, it is a whole error (the full payment or the full HCC coefficient).
  5. Audited units keep their own findings.
  6. Rerun the payment function for every unit (the risk score × county rate, the Part D benefit, APTC, or claim × taint). Errors are netted per unit, summed to over, under, gross, and net, and divided by X.

The estimate is the mean of the 1,000 iteration rates. The interval runs from the 2.5th to the 97.5th percentile of those rates.

Appendix B. Program designs and assumptions

Values marked illustrative are not published by CMS. These values are set to plausible levels and labeled as such in the configs.

B.1 Medicare Part C (Medicare Advantage risk adjustment)

Population (330,253 enrollees × 100):

  • 550 contracts with lognormal enrollment and a coding-intensity factor.
  • HCCs from 10 illustrative families, each with a mild and a severe level.
  • Payment = risk score × county rate × months, scaled to $388.72B of payments subject to risk adjustment.

Errors:

  • HCCs not supported by the record: deleted, or moved down the hierarchy (severe to mild).
  • Missing documentation (every coded HCC is lost).
  • HCCs supported by the record but not coded (underpayments).
  • Calibrated to the FY 2025 Part C results: gross 6.09%, overpayments $21.43B, underpayments $2.23B, missing documentation 0.22%.
  • The simulated truth is gross 6.06% ($23.57B).

Audit: 990 enrollees, 330 per national tercile of the submitted risk score (stratified SRS). The actual Part C IPM sample size and strata are not public, so the terciles follow the 2012 RADV design.

Estimators:

  • Expansion works in risk-score units: within each tercile, the mean over and under risk-score error × the tercile’s size × the national mean county rate × months.
  • Ratio uses the enrollee payment. Regression uses payment, demographic score, months enrolled, and number of HCCs within each tercile.

Bootstrap simulation:

  • One failure rate per HCC (20). A failed audited instance is one that was deleted, moved, or on a record with missing documentation, and it loses its HCC’s mean audited loss.
  • One addition rate per family (10).
  • An HCC with no audited instance never fails (rates are not extrapolated to unaudited HCCs).

B.2 Medicare Part D (prescription drug event errors)

Population (108,000 beneficiaries × 500; about 4.5 million PDEs):

  • Gross drug cost (GDC) = ingredient cost + dispensing fee + sales tax + vaccine administration fee.
  • 4% of beneficiaries are in long-term care (LTC) and fill about twice as many PDEs (illustrative).
  • The CY 2025 post-IRA standard benefit is run on submitted and corrected PDEs:
    • $590 deductible and $2,000 out-of-pocket cap;
    • Manufacturer Discount Program;
    • 20% and 40% reinsurance;
    • low-income subsidy copays.
  • Payments are direct subsidy + reinsurance + LICS, $105.56B in all.

Errors:

  • One candidate per PDE. 90% are full errors (GDC → 0) and 10% are partial, with the error share uniform on [−100%, 100%).
  • Error probability is 10% for LTC PDEs and 2.5% for other PDEs (illustrative).
  • This population is not calibrated, and the truth is 1.38% gross ($1.46B).
  • The FY 2025 Part D result was 4.00%, so this population tests the method at a lower error rate than production.

Audits:

  • PDE audit of 5,000, in three strata: LTC; non-LTC in the lowest decile of GDC; other non-LTC. Neyman allocation.
    • Used by expansion and by the bootstrap simulation.
    • Expansion applies each stratum’s audited error rate to every PDE in the stratum and reruns the benefit. Its interval is a delta-method interval: each stratum rate is moved ±1 SE and the benefit rerun each way.
  • Beneficiary audit: an SRS of 990 beneficiaries with every PDE reviewed. Used by ratio and regression.

Because the two audits differ, the comparison between methods is a comparison between designs as well as estimators.

B.3 Medicaid PERM (fee-for-service and managed care, claims and eligibility)

Population (9 states, 3.09 million payments × 200; 264,565 beneficiaries):

  • FFS claims and monthly managed-care capitations.
  • Rates are measured at federal share, Σy·FMAP / Σx·FMAP, with $142.0B in federal payments.

Errors:

  • Claims-review errors: FFS 6.5% of payments (70% all-or-nothing, 30% partial); MC 0.3%.
  • Eligibility errors, set beneficiary by beneficiary. Each state’s true rate is set on a grid from 2.00% to 4.00%.
  • Gross = the whole payment if eligibility is in error, otherwise the claims error.
  • Calibrated to the Medicaid FY 2025 rate of 6.12%; the simulated truth is 6.09%.

Audit:

  • Strata within each state: payment type (FFS, MC) × five equal-count payment quintiles (10 strata).
  • Neyman allocation of 520 payments per state-year, with systematic selection after sorting by amount within stratum.
  • National pooling:
    • Analytic methods add the state dollar totals and variances.
    • The bootstrap simulation sums the states’ iteration totals iteration by iteration.

Bootstrap simulation:

  • Claims findings are matched within the payment stratum.
  • The eligibility error rate is taken from the audited beneficiaries of the state.

B.4 Marketplace APTC (EIPM, Federally-facilitated Exchange)

Population (142,152 applications × 100):

  • Households with income, size, age, and benchmark premium.
  • APTC under the enhanced applicable-percentage schedule for 2021–2025.
  • APTC recipients only.

Errors:

  • Because the FY 2023–2025 public results report no underpayments, every error is an overpayment.
  • Technical errors: a required data match was not run, so the whole APTC is improper. 48% of improper dollars, matching FY 2025 (0.43 of 0.89 points).
  • Monetary errors: the supported income is higher than the income used, by a lognormal factor, and APTC is recomputed. 52% of improper dollars.
  • The simulated truth is 0.92% gross, including technical 0.43%.

Audit:

  • Stratified SRS of 2,000 applications, matching CMS’s “approximately 2,000”.
  • Strata are five equal-count quintiles of annual APTC (illustrative; the EIPM strata are not public), with Neyman allocation.

Bootstrap simulation:

  • Within each quintile, an error is technical or monetary at the audited shares.
  • A monetary error transfers the donor’s ratio of supported to used income, and APTC is recomputed for the recipient, clipped to 100–600% FPL.

B.5 Medicare fee-for-service (CERT)

Population (5.34 million claims × 200), in four claim types:

Claim type Synthetic claims FY 2025 payments
Part A hospital IPPS 40,000 $118.5B
Part A excluding IPPS 900,000 $197.9B
Part B 4,000,000 $114.1B
DMEPOS 400,000 $9.4B
  • Amounts are lognormal within type (illustrative spread), scaled to FY 2025 payments ($439.9B in all).
  • MAC jurisdictions are not modeled.

Errors:

  • At most one error per claim.
  • Whole-claim overpayments: no or insufficient documentation, medical necessity, other. These are 92% of overpayment dollars.
  • Partial coding overpayments: 8% of overpayment dollars, sized 5–60% of the payment.
  • Coding underpayments: 5–50% of the payment.
  • Per-type rates are closed form, from FY 2025 overpayment and underpayment dollars (Supplemental Improper Payment Data, Tables A1 and A4).
  • The simulated truth is 6.50% gross (FY 2025: 6.55%), with $0.92B in underpayments (FY 2025: $1.0B).

Audit:

  • 37,651 claims, the FY 2025 count of claims reviewed per claim type: IPPS 8,750; Part A excluding IPPS 8,651; Part B 11,600; DMEPOS 8,650.
  • Neyman allocation across five equal-count payment quintiles within each type. These stand in for CERT’s roughly 100 service-level strata per type, which cannot be rebuilt from public data.
  • The production estimator reduces nationally to a separate ratio estimator.
  • The Part A-to-B rebilling adjustment is out of scope.

B.6 Assumptions common to all programs

  1. Review is perfect. The audit finding equals the truth, and there are no reviewer errors and no appeals.
  2. One audit per year. Each audit year has one audit, and every method uses only that audit.
  3. Errors are independent of selection. Within a stratum, error probability does not depend on whether a unit is sampled. In PERM, CERT, and EIPM, it also does not depend on payment size, except through the strata definitions.
  4. Neyman inputs. Allocations use the stratum standard deviations of the population as a stand-in for prior-year data (which production designs have but this study does not).
  5. Gross is the reported rate. PIIA gross rates are the primary estimand. Recovery, contract-level RADV extrapolation, and provider demands are out of scope.
  6. Unknown payments count as improper, following CMS practice (for example, missing documentation in Part C).
