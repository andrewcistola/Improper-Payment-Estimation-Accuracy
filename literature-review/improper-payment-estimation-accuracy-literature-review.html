Abstract

Background. The Payment Integrity Information Act of 2019 requires statistically valid improper payment estimates, with confidence intervals, for federal programs at risk of significant improper payments. The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) produces five such estimates: Medicare Part C, Medicare Part D, Medicaid and CHIP (PERM), the Marketplace advance premium tax credit (EIPM), and Medicare fee-for-service (CERT). Each estimate projects audited errors from a probability sample using either design-based analytic estimators (expansion, ratio, or regression) or simulation that reruns the payment rules.

Objective. The purpose of this review is to summarize the evidence comparing available methods for estimating improper payments in federal health programs. In this review, accuracy has two parts:

  • point accuracy;
  • interval accuracy (coverage at the nominal level and, after that, width).

Methods. This study is a narrative review of two bodies of work:

  • public statute, OMB guidance, and CMS, HHS, GAO, and OIG methodology documents for the five programs and for contract-level RADV audits;
  • the statistical, audit-sampling and survey-bootstrap literature on interval accuracy for skewed, zero-inflated payment errors.

Findings. All five programs use stratified probability samples and ratio-type estimators. Part D alone describes its estimate as simulation-based, although the interval algorithm itself is not public, while the others document or imply normal-theory intervals. Interval accuracy depends mainly on two factors (as opposed to the name of the method):

  • the skew of the error distribution;
  • the number of nonzero errors in the sample.

Normal-theory intervals are conservative at low error rates and liberal at high ones. Second-order bootstrap intervals (BCa and bootstrap-t) correct much of the low-error failure. No published study has compared these methods against a known truth in the designs CMS uses (risk-score, drug-benefit, eligibility, APTC, or national claims estimates).

Conclusions. The comparative accuracy of improper payment estimation methods in federal health programs is largely untested, and the methods in production rest on assumptions that the evidence calls into question. This review sets out a research agenda for design-based Monte Carlo validation against synthetic populations, scored on the metric that matches the use of each estimate.

Keywords: improper payments; payment integrity; survey sampling; bootstrap; confidence intervals; Medicare; Medicaid; audit sampling

1. Introduction

Every improper payment estimate in a federal health program does three things:

  1. draws a probability sample of payments;
  2. establishes the payment error of each sampled unit through review;
  3. projects the errors to the program with an uncertainty statement.

These estimates matter. The five CMS measurements reported more than $90 billion in improper payments for FY 2025, and their confidence intervals are audited by Inspectors General. In addition, in some settings the estimates become financial determinations (for example, Medicare Advantage contract recoveries and, beginning in FY 2030, Medicaid federal match disallowances above a 3% eligibility error rate).

The purpose of this review is to summarize the evidence comparing available methods for estimating improper payments in federal health programs. This review defines accuracy as in the companion Monte Carlo study:

  • Point accuracy: how close the estimate is to the true improper payment.
  • Interval accuracy: whether the 95% interval contains the truth in about 95% of repeated audits (coverage) and, among intervals that do, how narrow the interval is (width).

The review is organized around three questions:

  1. Terminology and practice. What are the estimation and extrapolation techniques, and how does each program deploy them?
  2. Evidence. What does the literature say about the accuracy and limitations of each technique?
  3. Fit. Where would simulation and bootstrap methods help or hurt in each program, and how should their accuracy be tested?

Two inferential targets run through every section and should be kept apart:

  • The national improper payment rate reported under the Payment Integrity Information Act, which is a program-level ratio with a two-sided confidence interval.
  • The contract- or entity-level recovery amount (for example, a RADV extrapolation against a Medicare Advantage contract). In this setting, the estimate becomes a legal demand and is usually taken at a lower confidence bound.

A method adequate for the first can still be inadequate for the second, because coverage failures in one tail matter more than overall width.

2. Review approach

This study is a narrative review (as opposed to a systematic review). Two bodies of evidence were searched and read in full where available:

  1. Program sources. Primary public sources on the design and estimation of each program, including statute and OMB guidance; CMS program pages, error-rate findings, methodology documents, and manuals; OMB information-collection supporting statements; GAO and OIG reports; and court records on RADV extrapolation.
  2. Methods literature. Statistical, audit-sampling and survey-methods literature on the accuracy of estimators and intervals for skewed and zero-inflated payment errors, including finite-population and survey bootstraps.

Sources were identified by targeted searches, from the references of key papers (especially Edwards, Liu and Delage 2023; Kvanli and Schauer n.d.; Mashreghi, Haziza and Léger 2016; the National Research Council panel of 1988), and from CMS methodology documents. Program facts are taken only from public documents current to October 2026, and anything that would require non-public contractor methodology plans is flagged as unknown. This review covers the PIIA national rate as its primary target and RADV recovery as context. Ninety-five sources are annotated in the reference list.

3. Regulatory and institutional background

Every estimate in scope exists because the Payment Integrity Information Act of 2019 (PIIA) requires a statistically valid improper payment estimate for programs at risk of significant improper payments, and Appendix C to Office of Management and Budget (OMB) Circular A-123 (M-21-19, March 5, 2021) sets the rules. Payments are “significant” above $10 million and 1.5% of outlays, or above $100 million outright (M-21-19, §II.C).

What Appendix C requires. Each program submits a Sampling and Estimation Methodology Plan (S&EMP) to OMB (Phase 2). OMB reviews the plan but does not formally approve it, and the guidance does not prescribe a confidence level; agencies are to “work with their statistician to determine the appropriate confidence interval.” Appendix C also defines the unknown payment: one the agency cannot classify as proper or improper because documentation is insufficient. Inspectors General review the plans annually, and interval accuracy is an audited item. For example, the Department of Education Office of Inspector General (OIG) has repeatedly found estimates unreliable because nonrandom student-level sampling made the confidence intervals inaccurate, a finding first reported for FY 2019 and repeated through the FY 2025 review (ED-OIG/A26DC0288, August 4, 2026). This precedent matters here because a change in interval method is something an OIG can test.

Precision targets. Medicare FFS (CERT) designs for at least a 95% confidence interval of ±3 percentage points (CMS CERT overview). PERM’s older statutory target was a 90% interval of ±2.5 points nationally, and CMS adds its own state-level goal of 95% ±3 points (MACPAC).

Who does the work. The Payment Accuracy and Reporting Group (PARG) in CMS’s Office of Financial Management runs improper payment measurement for Medicare FFS, Parts C and D, Medicaid, CHIP, and APTC, across 11 contracts and about $114 million a year (PARG director bio; no primary CMS budget document states these figures). One boundary is important to note. The national Part C measure is a PARG product, but contract-specific RADV recovery audits are planned and extrapolated by CMS’s Center for Program Integrity (PY 2019 RADV methods). The two share review machinery but not inferential goals.

4. Terminology of error estimation and extrapolation

Every measure in scope does the same three things: draw a probability sample, establish a payment error for each sampled unit through review, and then project (“extrapolate”) to the frame and attach an uncertainty statement. The programs differ in the estimator, the variance method, and what the interval is used for. These three choices determine which method is most accurate in each setting.

Estimators

The expansion (mean-per-unit, Horvitz–Thompson) estimator weights each sampled error by the inverse of its selection probability. This is the estimator used by 2012 RADV and PIM Chapter 8 contractors, and it is the default in the HHS OIG’s RAT-STATS software, which is the de facto standard implementation of normal-theory bounds in Medicare integrity audits (HHS OIG, RAT-STATS). Standard textbook treatments are Cochran (1977) and Lohr (2022).

The ratio estimator divides projected improper dollars by projected paid dollars. Improper payment rates (PERM, Part C, Part D, APTC) are ratios, so their variance comes from linearization or replication (as opposed to the simple formula above). Current RADV (PY 2019 onward) estimates the mean change in risk score and then multiplies it by the summed county rates of the frame, which is effectively a ratio-type estimator.

Difference and regression estimators use the known paid amount x as an auxiliary variable. These estimators are standard in audit sampling (Kaplan 1973 was the first to show that the zero/nonzero mixture of audit populations undermines their normal-theory intervals), but are not documented in the public PARG methods. The choice between stratified expansion and ratio estimation in Medicare and Medicaid benefit integrity investigations is treated directly by Edwards (2011), which appears to be the only published treatment of that choice for these audits. Valliant, Dorfman and Royall (2000) give the prediction (model-based) view of these estimators, which is the frame in which the auxiliary information of the full population can be used; this view also describes what the simulation methods below do. Composite estimators combine separately estimated components. For example, the PERM overall rate is claims rate plus eligibility rate minus an overlap term, and the national rate rolls three state cycles together.

Simulation-based estimation

A third family of methods simulates (as opposed to projects). In audit-finding simulation, the audit findings are assigned at random to the unaudited units of the whole population, the program’s payment function is rerun, and the process is repeated many times. The findings are which HCCs failed, which drug claims were wrong and by how much, and which applications were ineligible. The estimate and interval are read from the distribution of simulated improper payments.

This method is attractive for three reasons:

  • Nonlinear payments. The simulation reruns the actual payment rules (for example, Part D benefit phases, risk-score hierarchies, and the APTC schedule).
  • Every measure at once. One set of iterations produces over-, under-, gross, and net payments, along with any subgroup.
  • Traceability. Every simulated dollar can be traced to an audited finding.

This method differs from the survey bootstraps in the table above in one important way: the audit itself is not resampled. Two versions are possible, and this review names them differently because only one of them is a bootstrap in the usual sense:

  • Plug-in audit-finding simulation (fixed rates). The error rates estimated from the audit are reused in every iteration. Nothing that was observed is resampled, so the method is a plug-in microsimulation (a stochastic reconstruction of the population under point-estimated rates) and not a bootstrap. Its spread measures only the variability of reassigning findings across the population.
  • Parametric-bootstrap audit-finding simulation (parametric rates). The rates are redrawn from their sampling distribution (for example, binomial) before each iteration, which represents the uncertainty of the audit itself. This is a parametric bootstrap of the rates (Efron and Tibshirani 1993, ch. 6).

The distinction is the same one that separates single from multiple imputation. A single imputation treats the imputation model as known and understates variance by the between-imputation component; multiple imputation redraws the model parameters and recovers it (Rubin 1987). By the law of total variance, the plug-in version omits the between-audit component in the same way, so its interval is expected to be too narrow by an amount that grows as the population grows relative to the audit. The parametric version is a frequentist device, as opposed to the Bayesian bootstrap of Rubin (1981), which places a posterior on the observed values rather than on a parametric rate. Whether the interval from either version holds its nominal coverage is an empirical question addressed in Sections 6 and 8.

Decision quantities and performance language

  • Lower confidence bound (LB). PIM Chapter 8 recovers the lower limit of a one-sided 90% interval; the 2012 RADV method used the lower limit of a two-sided 99% interval (one-sided 99.5%); PY 2019–2021 RADV use the lower limit of a 90% interval only as a gate and recover the point estimate. The PY 2019 bound uses z = 1.645, which is the lower limit of a two-sided 90% interval and therefore a one-sided 95% bound; the PY 2021 document specifies t(n−1) without stating whether the 90% is one- or two-sided. The difference between the two readings is a frequent source of confusion in appeals. Gilliland and Edwards (2011) propose randomized confidence limits as one way to balance the risk between the government and the provider in this setting.
  • Coverage and one-sided non-coverage. The share of repeated samples in which the interval (or bound) contains the true total. For recovery, the operative metric is how often LB exceeds the truth, nominally 10% for a one-sided 90% bound.
  • Viability and recovery percent. Edwards et al. call a design “viable” if it achieves at least 89% coverage at every tested error rate, and measure efficiency as average recovery as a share of the true overpayment.
  • Monte Carlo vs bootstrap. Monte Carlo here means simulating from a model or a synthetic population (used to validate designs and to propagate component uncertainty). Bootstrap means resampling the observed sample, or redrawing a parameter from its estimated sampling distribution. CMS’s Part D documents use “simulation” for both the benefit re-adjudication and the interval iterations, so the word alone does not indicate which is meant. A Monte Carlo study of an interval method is itself a statistical experiment, and its coverage estimates carry Monte Carlo error that should be reported with them (Koehler, Brown and Haneuse 2009; Morris, White and Crowther 2019).

Medicare Part C: national measure and contract audits

The Part C IPM verifies that HCCs submitted for payment are supported in the medical record for a sampled enrollee, computes the enrollee-level payment error, and projects this error to the Part C population subject to risk adjustment (CMS Part C IPM). The FY 2025 rate was 6.09% ($23.67B gross, $19.20B net) on a $388.72B denominator, and missing documentation was only 0.22% of expenditures (FY 2025 results). One definitional change affected the series: “spontaneous” additional HCCs were removed from the underpayment calculation beginning in CY 2020. The results document does not state the sample size, the strata or the interval method.

Medicare Part D

The Part D IPM samples beneficiaries, requests documentation for their PDEs, corrects gross drug cost where the record does not support the PDE, and runs the corrected PDEs through a simulation of the benefit year so that errors flow through benefit phases and reinsurance (CMS Part D IPM; HHS performance plan). The program’s background page states that the validation findings “are applied to a randomly selected 5% sample of the Part D beneficiary population,” that the payment error is calculated for that sample, and that the result is extrapolated to the remaining beneficiaries (Part D IPM program background). This 5% sample is the extrapolation base (as opposed to the audited sample), and it is the closest public description of an audit-finding simulation in any of the five programs. The FY 2023 redesign changed the sampling unit, reduced “the number of simulation iterations to align to statistical literature,” and aligned the confidence interval to the estimation method (FY 2023 results). In FY 2025, the program moved from cohort to random beneficiary selection.

The published intervals are slightly asymmetric in FY 2023 ($2.63B–$4.17B around $3.35B, or −0.72/+0.82) and nearly symmetric in FY 2025 ($3.77B–$4.71B around $4.23B, or −0.46/+0.48) (FY 2025 results), so the published intervals do not by themselves reveal the algorithm. Neither findings document states that the interval is read from the simulation iterations. Public documents do not name the algorithm, so the internal S&EMP is the authority on this point. Missing or invalid documentation was 75% of sampled errors in FY 2025, which makes the error distribution closer to all-or-nothing than to small pricing discrepancies.

Medicaid and CHIP (PERM)

PERM measures each state once every three years and builds a national rolling rate from the latest three cycles (PERM Manual, April 2025). Within a state, quarterly claims universes are stratified by payment (five payment strata for managed care in recent cycles; RY 2022 kickoff) and, per the OMB supporting statement (OMB control no. 0938-0974; ICR 202408-0938-026; not independently verified for this review), selected systematically within payment strata after sorting by paid amount. Preston (2009) gives a rescaled bootstrap for stratified multistage designs of this kind. Each component rate is projected improper dollars over projected paid dollars, and the overall rate adds the eligibility rate to the claims rate and subtracts their overlap (CMS rate calculation).

The stakes for state-level estimates changed in 2025. P.L. 119-21 §71106 (July 4, 2025) disallows federal match on eligibility improper payments above a 3% rate starting October 1, 2029, and removes the good-faith waiver at 42 CFR 431.1010 (statute; summarized by BPC 2026). A state eligibility rate near 3% now carries a dollar consequence, so its interval behaves like a recovery bound (as opposed to only a reporting statistic). RY 2027 measures eligibility only (RY 2027 kickoff).

Marketplace APTC (EIPM)

EIPM reviews a stratified random sample of about 2,000 FFE applications to test whether eligibility determinations and APTC amounts were correct (EIPM background; CMS 2022 release). Rates have stayed near or below 1% (0.62% in the first FY 2022 report; 0.58% in FY 2023; 1.01% in FY 2024; 0.89% in FY 2025, margin of error ±0.54 points). No underpayments were reported for FY 2023–2025 (PaymentAccuracy.gov). In FY 2025, 0.43 of the 0.89 points were technically improper payments, where a required periodic data match was not run (CMS EIPM improper payment findings). The remainder came from manual errors (for example, income entered incorrectly or documents accepted despite name or date-of-birth discrepancies). At this rate, a 2,000-unit sample contains only a few dozen errors, which is the rare-error setting of Cox and Snell (1979) and the setting in which normal-theory bounds are least reliable. State-based Exchanges come under a separate SEIPM rule (45 CFR 155 Subpart Q) with state-specific sample sizes and a three-year cycle, applicable beginning January 1, 2027 (45 CFR §155.1605).

Medicare fee-for-service (CERT)

CERT reviews a stratified random sample of Medicare FFS claims submitted from July through June, including both paid and denied claims (CERT introduction).

  • Strata. The first level is claim type (Part A hospital IPPS, Part A excluding IPPS, Part B, and DMEPOS). Within each type, there are about 100 service-level strata, and Part A excluding IPPS uses two-digit type-of-bill strata, fewer than 20 (CERT methodology).
  • Estimator. A generalized estimator produces national, contractor-cluster, and service-level rates. At the national level it reduces to the separate ratio estimator, with contractor clusters weighted by their share of expenditures.
  • FY 2025 sample. 49,061 claims were sampled and 37,651 reviewed: IPPS 8,750; Part A excluding IPPS 8,651; Part B 11,600; DMEPOS 8,650.
  • FY 2025 results. The gross rate was 6.55% (95% CI 5.9–7.2%), with $27.9B in overpayments and $1.0B in underpayments, counted gross (2025 Supplemental Improper Payment Data). By type, the rates ranged from 3.1% (IPPS) to 24.1% (DMEPOS).
  • Error categories. Insufficient documentation accounted for 53% of improper dollars, medical necessity 15%, no documentation 12%, incorrect coding 11%, and other 8.5%.

Most CERT errors are therefore whole-claim, all-or-nothing errors, with partial errors coming mainly from coding. This is the error setting studied by Edwards, Liu and Delage (2023), but at a national sample size where the central limit theorem has far more to work with than in a 30-claim provider audit.

6. Evidence on comparative accuracy

The literature converges on one rule: whether an interval holds its nominal coverage depends mainly on the skew of the error distribution and the number of nonzero errors in the sample (as opposed to the name of the method). Bootstrap intervals correct the low-error, right-skew failure well. However, they have barely been tested in the high-error, all-or-nothing setting that Medicare reviews actually produce.

Normal-theory intervals

  • The principle of skew. A right-skewed population produces a conservative lower bound and a liberal upper bound, and a left-skewed population reverses this pattern (Cochran 1977, pp. 39–44, as applied by Edwards, Liu & Delage 2023). Overpayment populations are a spike at zero plus a payment-shaped tail, so their skew reverses as the error rate rises.
  • Low error rates: too conservative. With few nonzero errors, normal lower limits sit far below the truth. In Kvanli and Schauer’s simulations, the traditional 90% interval’s lower limit exceeded the true total in only 0.3–2.3% of samples as opposed to a nominal 5% (Kvanli and Schauer n.d.). Neter and Loebbecke’s 1975 AICPA study of real accounting populations is the classic early demonstration that standard estimators’ intervals miss nominal coverage when errors are rare, and Kaplan (1973) had already identified the mixture structure as the cause. Cox and Snell (1979) give the canonical model for sampling and estimating rare errors, and Rohrbach (1993) proposes augmenting the variance estimate so that the interval reaches nominal coverage in audit populations, which is a direct precursor of the coverage problem this review addresses.
  • High error rates: too liberal. In 166 Medicare integrity samples, more than 76% of payments were in error and the data fit an all-or-nothing model. Under this model, a simple random sample of 30 fell below 90% confidence once the error rate passed 0.6 for two of three test populations, and no stratification algorithm except the geometric (Geo) algorithm of Gunning and Horgan (2004) was viable for more than about half of the 104 test populations; the geometric algorithm reached 88% in one design cell (Edwards et al. 2023). At very high error rates, the bound can even exceed total payments.
  • Stratification can make it worse. Payment-based strata can create left-skewed overpayment strata at high error rates, and King and Madansky (2013) warn that careless stratification can invalidate the confidence coefficient itself. The stratification of skewed payment populations is reviewed by Horgan (2006).
  • CMS acknowledges the gap. PIM Chapter 8 accepts “standard methods” from texts and journals but acknowledges that 90% coverage cannot be guaranteed in every audit (PIM Ch. 8, §8.4.5).

Bootstrap intervals

  • Theory. Percentile intervals are first-order accurate, while BCa and bootstrap-t are second-order accurate, correcting for skewness at rate 1/n rather than 1/√n (Hall 1988; Efron 1987; DiCiccio & Efron 1996). In small samples, the percentile interval is too narrow, and bootstrap-t handles skew better (Hesterberg 2015).
  • Audit populations with rare errors. Kvanli and Schauer found BCa lower limits exceeded the truth in 3.2–4.5% of samples (nominal 5%), much closer than the traditional method, with average lower limits 5.5–531% higher depending on the expected number of errors. The gain fades after about 30 expected errors in the sample, at which point both methods agree; the authors conclude that the expected number of errors, as opposed to a minimum count of nonzero errors actually observed, is what drives the gain. On twelve real tax-audit samples, BCa lower limits were 3–548% higher. Earlier bootstrap bounds for audit populations are Biddle, Bruton and Siegel (1990), who bootstrapped the difference and ratio estimators, and Howard (1994), who combined Hoeffding’s inequality with the bootstrap to obtain a bound for overstatement error.
  • Survey designs. Rescaled bootstraps reproduce design variance under stratified sampling (Rao & Wu 1988; Rao, Wu & Yue 1992), building on the first bootstrap for stratified samples by Bickel and Freedman (1984). Simulation comparisons ranked coverage bootstrap > BRR > jackknife > linearization, with the bootstrap best for one-sided intervals (Krewski & Rao 1981; Rao & Wu 1988; Kovar, Rao & Wu 1988; together with a 1996 review by Shao that was not consulted directly, as summarized collectively by Kolenikov 2008). Sitter (1992b) compares three survey bootstraps and finds none dominant. A naive iid bootstrap ignores the finite population correction and overstates variance (Mashreghi, Haziza & Léger 2016).
  • Rare-error refinements. Helmers (2000) combines a Cornish–Fisher expansion with bootstrap calibration to get upper limits for total error when errors are rare and highly skewed (Biometrika). Berger, Chiodini and Zenga (2021) give an adjusted empirical likelihood bound for monetary-unit sampling that is designed for the same regime.
  • Skewed health spending. The cost-effectiveness literature adopted the nonparametric bootstrap for skewed per-person costs (Barber & Thompson 2000), a close analogue to per-enrollee payment error.

Source: Kvanli & Schauer (n.d., unpublished manuscript hosted by the Multistate Tax Commission), Tables 1–3 · 15 population–sample-size cells, minimum 3 nonzero errors per sample.

Even at 60 expected errors, the normal-theory bound misses less than half as often as it should, so it under-recovers. In contrast, BCa remains slightly conservative throughout. Populations had 2%, 5%, and 10% error rates with exponential error sizes and 5,000 samples per cell.

Where the bootstrap is expected to fail or is untested:

  1. Few or no nonzero errors. If the sample has zero or one error, every resample is degenerate and the bound collapses to zero or to the single error. EIPM-scale error rates and small RADV contracts are at risk.
  2. Bounded, all-or-nothing errors at high rates. Resampling cannot produce values outside the observed support and inherits the skew of the sample. Whether this corrects the normal bound’s liberal behavior at error rates above 0.6 has not been published for Medicare data.
  3. Tiny strata and systematic samples. Rescaling requires at least two units per stratum, and the sorted systematic selection used by PERM has no exact design-based variance, so any bootstrap treats it as implicitly stratified.
  4. Model-ranked frames. RADV PY 2019+ frames are chosen by a prediction model, so inference applies only to the frame. A bootstrap inside the frame is valid, but extrapolating its interval beyond the frame is not.
  5. Composite and multi-phase estimators. Rolling PERM rates and claims-plus-eligibility composites require resampling each phase and component on its own design, and then recombining them. Correlations between components (the overlap term) must be carried through.
  6. Reproducibility. Tail quantiles need thousands of replicates, and appeals require that the result be replicable, so the seed, replicate count, and Monte Carlo error must be documented as PIM §8.4.4.2 already requires for sample selection.

The gap this review identifies. No published study compares the accuracy of the available methods against a known truth in the settings CMS measures:

  • RADV-style risk-score extrapolation;
  • Part D PDE-to-payment simulation;
  • PERM eligibility rates;
  • APTC errors;
  • the national CERT claims estimate.

This gap applies to analytic, bootstrap, and audit-finding simulation methods alike. The closest evidence comes from Medicare FFS provider audits (high error, all-or-nothing) and tax and accounting audits (low error, right skew). The CMS programs span both settings. In addition, before the companion study the validity of audit-finding simulation intervals, whether plug-in or parametric-bootstrap, had not been tested. 7. Fit of simulation and bootstrap methods by program

The case for the bootstrap is strongest where samples are small, errors are skewed, and an interval drives a dollar decision (RADV first, then PERM state eligibility rates, and then EIPM). Part D already relies on simulation, so the question for Part D is validation (as opposed to adoption). Part C IPM gains the least.

RADV contract audits

Benefits.

  • At n = 35 the normal or t bound rests entirely on the CLT for a skewed, discrete variable (change in risk score). Bootstrap-t and BCa correct the skewness term the t bound ignores.
  • The PY 2021 estimator is a ratio over enrollee-months. Resampling enrollees (with all their months and HCCs) produces its variance without the linearization step.
  • Resampling can bound the dollar payment error directly, including county-rate weighting (as opposed to bounding the mean risk-score change and multiplying afterward).

Drawbacks.

  • The decision rule is one-sided: extrapolate if the bound exceeds zero, and then recover the point estimate. What matters is the false-extrapolation rate when the true mean change is at or below zero. At n = 35, percentile intervals are known to be too narrow, which would raise this rate.
  • If the targeted frame yields mostly right-skewed changes (many zeros, some large HCC deletions), a better-calibrated bound can be higher than the t bound, as in Kvanli and Schauer. This would shift recoveries upward and would likely invite challenge.
  • Inference applies only to the prediction-model frame, and the bootstrap cannot resolve the frame definition or the FFS-adjuster dispute.
  • A method change during litigation is itself a legal event, so any bootstrap must be fully replicable from a documented seed.

Implementation. Each contract should be treated as SRS without replacement from its frame, using a pseudo-population or rescaled bootstrap so that the FPC is honored when n/N is large (frames can be as small as 30). Enrollees should be resampled as clusters, the month-weighted mean change and the dollar estimate computed in each replicate, and bootstrap-t and BCa bounds formed with at least 5,000 replicates. The probability that the bound exceeds zero should be reported alongside the bound.

Part C IPM

Benefits. Replicate weights give one variance method for gross, net, over- and underpayment rates and for HCC-level or plan-type domains. Drawbacks. The national sample is large and stratified, so the CLT should hold, and coverage gains will likely be small. Implementation. The recommended approach is a Rao–Wu–Yue rescaled bootstrap within risk strata (resampling nₕ−1 per stratum), with percentile and BCa intervals compared against the current method. This would be useful mainly as a cross-check and for domains.

Part D IPM

Benefits. The estimator is a nonlinear simulation (corrected PDEs re-run through benefit phases and reinsurance), so linearization is impractical, and resampling is the natural variance method. CMS describes the estimate as simulation-based and has changed the number of simulation iterations, but the published intervals do not reveal whether the interval is read from those iterations, and if it is, whether the audited rates are held fixed (a plug-in simulation) or redrawn (a parametric bootstrap). This is the first thing a validation should establish, because the companion study finds that the plug-in version does not hold its nominal coverage.

Drawbacks and questions to settle.

  • What is resampled. Resampling beneficiaries after their payment errors are computed is inexpensive and valid if the simulated error of each beneficiary is fixed. Re-running the benefit simulation inside each replicate is only needed if the simulation itself has stochastic inputs.
  • Replicate count. The FY 2023 cut in simulation iterations needs a Monte Carlo error target for the 2.5% and 97.5% quantiles (as opposed to only the standard error).
  • Design fidelity. The FY 2025 switch from cohort to random beneficiary selection should be mirrored in the resampling scheme (strata, FPC).
  • Benefit redesign. The Inflation Reduction Act’s 2025 Part D redesign (including the $2,000 out-of-pocket cap) changes the benefit simulation for payment years from 2025, reported from FY 2027.

Implementation. The current method should be validated (as opposed to replaced) by building a synthetic beneficiary population from historical PDE and error data, repeating the full sampling-review-simulation pipeline, and measuring the coverage and tail balance of the current interval against BCa and bootstrap-t.

PERM

Benefits.

  • The overall rate is a composite (FFS + managed care + eligibility − overlap, at federal share), and the national rate pools three cycles. Replicate weights carry every covariance through without hand-derived formulas.
  • Beginning in FY 2030, a state eligibility rate above 3% triggers disallowance. With a few hundred cases per state, a one-sided bound near 3% needs calibrated coverage, and whether the statute’s “rate” means the point estimate or a bound is a rulemaking question worth raising.

Drawbacks. Systematic selection after sorting by paid amount has no exact variance estimator. In addition, eligibility cases are a second phase attached to sampled payments, difference resolution and appeals change findings after sampling, and the three cycles measure different years.

Implementation. Replicate weights should be generated per state at selection time, treating sorted systematic samples as implicitly stratified (paired adjacent units). The eligibility phase should then be resampled within the sampled payments, and each component and the composite computed per replicate. Because states are sampled independently, national replicates come from summing state replicate totals.

EIPM (APTC)

Benefits. At an error rate under 1%, a 2,000-application sample sits squarely in the rare-error setting where normal upper bounds are liberal and lower bounds are conservative, and BCa helps both tails. SBE measurement beginning in 2027 will produce smaller state samples where this matters more.

Drawbacks. Strata with zero observed errors produce degenerate resamples, and household-level APTC errors are bounded by the monthly APTC and may be all-or-nothing when eligibility fails.

Implementation. The recommended approach is a stratified rescaled bootstrap with BCa and bootstrap-t, with strata that have zero or one error flagged and compared against empirical likelihood and a zero-inflated parametric bound.

8. Research agenda: validating comparative accuracy

The strongest design is a design-based Monte Carlo study. Such a study would build realistic finite populations for each program, rerun the program’s actual sampling design and estimator thousands of times, and score every interval method against the known truth on the metric that matches how the interval is used. This is the approach Edwards et al. use for FFS audits, extended to bootstrap and audit-finding simulation comparators and to the five CMS designs. The study should be planned and reported in the ADEMP form (aims, data-generating mechanisms, estimands, methods, performance measures) of Morris, White and Crowther (2019), with the Monte Carlo standard error of every coverage estimate reported (Koehler, Brown and Haneuse 2009).

Study pipeline

  1. Build populations. Unit-level error models are fit to historical review outcomes: HCC-level validation probabilities and hierarchy moves for Part C and RADV; PDE-level documentation failure and pricing discrepancy for Part D; case-level eligibility error for PERM; application-level APTC error for EIPM. These models are imposed on real frame covariates (payments, risk scores, and county rates) to form “plasmode” populations, and stress populations are added on an error-rate grid from 0.01 to 0.95 with right-, left-, and two-spike payment shapes.
  2. Apply the real design. Frame rules (including the RADV prediction-model frame), strata, sample sizes, and selection mechanics are reproduced (sorted systematic selection for PERM; cohort versus random beneficiary for Part D).
  3. Apply the production estimator. The production estimator is applied along with plausible alternatives (ratio vs expansion; enrollee-month weighting).
  4. Compute every interval method. Every interval method (listed below) is computed on each simulated sample.
  5. Score. Each interval is scored against the population truth, and the process is repeated 10,000 times per scenario cell.
  6. Stress-test. Results are stress-tested with review measurement error (for example, coder disagreement and overturns on appeal), missing records scored as errors, and frame misspecification.

Comparators

Current analytic interval (z and t, Satterthwaite df where stratified); naive bootstrap percentile; design (rescaled or pseudo-population) bootstrap with percentile, basic, bootstrap-t, and BCa intervals; calibrated (double) bootstrap; plug-in and parametric-bootstrap audit-finding simulation; empirical likelihood; and, for RADV, a hypergeometric or minimum-sum bound as a conservative floor.

With 10,000 replications, the Monte Carlo standard error of a 10% exceedance rate is 0.3 percentage points. Viable methods can then be ranked by recovery or width.

Computation

A bootstrap inside a Monte Carlo study multiplies costs: 10,000 outer replications × 5,000 resamples is 5 × 10⁷ estimator evaluations per cell. Three practices keep this tractable: precomputing unit-level errors so that each resample is a weighted sum, generating multinomial or rescaled weights in vectorized blocks, and running cells as independent batch jobs over Parquet-staged populations. The warp-speed method of Giacomini, Politis and White (2013) can screen percentile-type intervals with one resample per outer replication before full runs, but it does not cover BCa.

Data and access needs

The study would be strongest with:

  • Methodology plans. The programs’ Sampling and Estimation Methodology Plans (sample sizes, strata, interval algorithms, replicate counts) and the PERM statistical contractor’s variance specification.
  • Historical review outcomes, at the unit level, held under appropriate data use terms. These outcomes would be used to calibrate error models.
  • Agreed metrics. A primary metric for each program, agreed with the reporting and recovery offices.

Without these, a study can still proceed on synthetic populations calibrated to public aggregates, with illustrative strata where the actual strata are not published. This is the approach of the companion Monte Carlo evaluation, which implements the first stage of this agenda: analytic estimators (expansion, ratio and regression) versus plug-in and parametric-bootstrap audit-finding simulation, at production error rates, with 25 audit years per program. It does not yet cover the survey bootstraps, BCa, bootstrap-t, empirical likelihood, the hypergeometric bounds, the one-sided recovery setting, or the error-rate grid above. The companion study found that plug-in audit-finding simulation intervals are invalid: they measure only the variability of reassigning findings across the population and shrink as the population grows relative to the audit. Redrawing the audited rates from their binomial sampling distribution (a parametric bootstrap of the rates) restored coverage for the gross rate. The parametric-bootstrap interval was also narrower than the ratio estimator’s, but that width advantage is model-based (it depends on errors being independent of payment size within strata, which was true of the synthetic populations by construction) and is being tested with size-dependent error scenarios before any recommendation rests on it.

9. Conclusions

The methods CMS uses to estimate improper payments are well established in survey sampling, but their accuracy in these settings is largely untested:

  • Ratio-type analytic estimators dominate production, with normal-theory intervals.
  • Part D relies on simulation.

The existing evidence points in a consistent direction. Interval accuracy depends on the skew of the errors and the number of nonzero errors in the sample. Normal-theory intervals are conservative when errors are rare and liberal when they are frequent. Second-order bootstrap intervals correct much of the rare-error failure but have barely been tested where errors are frequent and all-or-nothing.

Audit-finding simulation is attractive for nonlinear payment rules. However, its intervals had not been validated before the companion study. Because the plug-in version does not resample the audit, it understates uncertainty in the same way a single imputation does, and the companion study’s first-stage results confirm that its intervals are invalid, while the parametric-bootstrap version restores coverage for the gross rate.

The comparative accuracy of these methods should therefore be established empirically before any of them is relied on for new uses. The appropriate design is a Monte Carlo evaluation against populations with known truth, run under each program’s actual design and scored on the metric that matches how the estimate is used. These new uses include smaller audits, state-level PERM disallowances, State-based Exchange measurement, and contract-level recovery.
